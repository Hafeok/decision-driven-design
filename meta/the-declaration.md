# The declaration

**Status:** draft for Emil's ratification. Not filed. Drafted by Claude, 2026-08-24, from the
2026-08-23/24 working conversation.
**Purpose:** one problem statement, three registers — the repository README, Paper A's opening, and
the primer's §1. Written once so the three do not drift into three framings of one question.
**Constraint:** it is a problem statement, not a thesis and not a pitch. It reaches a boundary before
it reaches a promise.
**Sequencing:** ratify now; land with the primer (sequence item 6), in the settled vocabulary. If the
ground migration moves a term used below, the declaration migrates with it.

---

## A. The canonical text (~210 words)

> **Who does the work?**
>
> Every organisation has had to answer this, and the instruments for answering it all make the same
> move: where you cannot check the work, check the worker. Grade, seniority, licence, certification
> — each is a standing claim about a supplier, used where the output itself could not be verified
> directly.
>
> That move worked while there was one kind of answerer. There are now two, and the instruments do
> not transfer. A licence is worth something because the licensed person outlives the claims they
> make; model versions do not. A grade is informative because human capability is broadly
> correlated; model capability is not — it is superhuman in one region and incompetent in the one
> beside it. And a credential is issued by an institution that carries stake; a benchmark issues
> nothing and answers for nothing.
>
> The question needs a smaller unit. Work decomposes into decisions. Ask, for each decision, what
> must be determined, whether there is a check that closes, and from where the determination is
> supplied. Every determination can still be supplied by a person; that option never goes away. What
> changes is that the choice is made per decision rather than per job — and each way of supplying a
> determination carries a different cost, a different assurance, and a different answer to who is
> accountable when it is wrong. The framework does not tell you which to choose. It tells you what
> you are choosing between, and it makes the choice one you make on the record.

## B. The register variants

Each carries A verbatim, then diverges in exactly one paragraph.

### B-1 — repository README (the front door)

After A, one paragraph of what the repository is and what its labels mean:

> This repository is the framework that follows from taking that seriously: a claim graph in which
> every claim carries the observation that would break it. Most of it is `projected` — argued, not
> yet tested — and the status labels mean what they say. Two claims have been retired on the record.
> What follows is the current state of an argument, not a settled account.

**Nothing else above the fold.** No feature list, no adoption pitch, no "used by". The concession is
load-bearing: a stranger who meets a confident opening and then finds `projected` on the central
claims feels misled; one who is told first reads the same labels as discipline.

### B-2 — Paper A's opening (the argument)

After A, one paragraph naming the paper's own scope and deferring the answer to the body:

> This paper states the unit and the apparatus that follows from it: what a decision is, when a
> determination has been supplied and by what, what remains irreducible, and what may be claimed
> about an arrangement that mixes both kinds of answerer. It does not settle whether the
> quantitative extension is the right one, and says where that remains open.

This directly answers the external review's structural criticism — that the paper begins answering
before it has stated the question it answers.

### B-3 — the primer §1 (the manual)

After A, one paragraph on what a practitioner does with it:

> What follows is procedural: how to file a decision so it can be found, how to read an act and see
> what actually governed it, how to tell whether a check closes for the arrangement you have, and how
> to find the decisions nobody made. Each procedure states what it does not cover.

## C. What the declaration deliberately does not say

Recorded so the three registers cannot quietly acquire it:

- **It does not claim novelty.** The decomposition's ancestry is a survey the framework has not
  completed (`meta/lineage-and-limits.md` carries what is established).
- **It does not promise a measurement.** No mention of entropy, bits, or quantification: the
  measure is a candidate extension with an open construct-validity question, and the front door is
  the wrong place to imply otherwise.
- **It does not rank the two kinds of answerer.** The question is routing and supply. Nothing here
  licenses assessment of a person, and the profiling boundary holds: capability inference is a
  routing instrument under an authorising decision, never an evaluation of an individual.
- **It does not claim that any determination must be supplied by a model, or that the decomposition
  selects a supplier.** Every determination can be supplied by a person; the decomposition makes the
  choice answerable per decision and prices the alternatives. It does not make the choice. Recorded
  because an earlier draft's closing sentence implied otherwise (Emil's correction, 2026-08-24), and
  because the implication is the one most likely to creep back across three consumers.
- **It does not name the framework's primary contribution.** Two ratified superlatives compete in
  the repository (`f3-deferral.md`) and the declaration must not settle that by implication —
  which is also why it says *the unit*, not *the framework's best result*.

## D. Open for Emil

1. **"Who does the work?" versus "Who is performing the act?"** The draft takes the first: *act* is a
   technical term the reader does not have yet, and the opening should use no vocabulary it has not
   earned. If the second is wanted, the third paragraph must introduce *act* explicitly.
2. **Whether the second paragraph's three asymmetries are the right three.** Identity persistence,
   capability shape, accountability. A fourth candidate — that a model's supplier can change under
   you without notice — is arguably part of the first.
3. **The word `decisions`.** It is the framework's most contested term with an outside reader, who
   will hear "choices a person makes". The alternative — leading with *determinations* — is more
   precise and less legible. The draft chooses legibility and lets §2 do the precision.
4. **Whether B-1's concession paragraph is too much for a README.** It is the paragraph most likely
   to be softened later; recording that prediction is the point of naming it here.
