## Q44 — Two ontologies, and what each amortises over

### (a) The distinction

**Proposed.** The governing decisions of an act draw their determinables from **two different
ontologies**, and canon currently has one word for both:

- **The act ontology** — the distinctions that govern *how the work is done*. Which tool, in what
  sequence, by what technique. The carpenter's knowledge of saws, grain, joints and order.
- **The verdict ontology** — the distinctions that govern *what the output must be*. What a chair
  is, what makes one usable, what makes one good. The customer's knowledge, or the sector's.

Both are ontologies in the same sense — declared sets of determinables at a grain. What differs is
what each governs and, as (c) shows, what population each is shared with.

**Not identical to what/how.** That split is about *work*; this is about *where the distinctions come
from*. Related, and the boundary case in (e) is where they come apart.

**Falsifier:** a governing decision whose determinables cannot be assigned to either ontology, or
whose assignment is arbitrary across competent readers. If assignment has poor coding reliability,
the distinction is not carving a joint.

### (b) The verifiability asymmetry — a refinement of the floor

**Proposed, and this is the entry's sharpest content.**

Whether the *outcome* predicate closes and whether the *method* predicate closes are **separate
questions**, and canon has been treating them as one.

A chair can pass every inspection at handover and fail in two years because a joint was cut across
the grain. The output was verifiable; the method decision that determined its life was not — not
because no criterion exists, but because **its consequences fall outside the verdict's horizon.**

**Consequences:**

- The check-the-worker branch is reachable from *either* question. Craft traditions inspect the
  worker (apprenticeship, licensure) even where the product is inspectable, and this says why.
- It explains code review's existence without appealing to culture: you cannot tell from a running
  program whether its error handling was right, and the consequence arrives later than any verdict
  you render at merge.
- It gives the consequence-horizon material a second site: previously about assurance mechanisms
  outrunning their position, now also about *which ontology's decisions* can be assessed at all
  within a given horizon.

**Falsifier:** a domain where method decisions are reliably assessable from the output at the
verdict's own horizon — where nothing about how the work was done leaves a consequence the output
does not reveal in time.

### (b2) Assessing method, and why craft learns slowly

**Recorded, not separately claimed.** Assessing an act *against the act ontology* — was this the
right technique, in the right order — is an assessment act (Q39) whose determinables come from the
act ontology. The regress cuts already apply: depth-one self-supplied ground, constructive closure
where the criterion computes, accountable grounding where it does not. No new machinery.

What is worth naming is the difference from learning. Method-assessment yields a verdict about *this
act*; learning is the return channel, verdicts accumulating into encoding. Method verdicts update
the **act ontology itself** — the return channel pointed at the carving rather than at the rules —
and by (b) that signal arrives late, sparsely, and frequently attributed to the wrong cause. Which is
a mechanism for why craft improves more slowly than outcome-driven work, without appealing to
tradition or temperament.

### (c) What each ontology amortises over

**Proposed, downstream (economics).** The two ontologies are shared with different populations, and
that is what makes one a standing asset and the other an engagement:

| Layer | Shared with | Amortises over |
|---|---|---|
| Determination itself — how governing works at all | everyone | every arrangement |
| **Act ontology**, craft-scoped | everyone practising that craft on that stack | every engagement the practice runs |
| **Verdict ontology**, sector-scoped | everyone in the sector | every customer in that sector |
| **Tolerances and resolutions** | nobody | this customer, this engagement |

**The sharp form, in Johnson's terms:** within a sector the *determinables* are largely shared —
which dimensions matter is set by the sector. What is customer-specific is the **grain and the
determinates**: how tight the tolerance, and which alternative was chosen. Same axes, different
declarations.

**Consequence:** sector specialisation is not a relationship strategy. It is where the verdict
ontology amortises. A practice spanning unrelated sectors has as many verdict ontologies as sectors
and no sharing across them, exactly as a practice spanning unrelated stacks has as many act
ontologies.

### (d) Why sectors share — the mechanism

**Proposed, and it is testable.** A sector's verdict ontology is shareable to the extent the sector
has **shared institutional ground**: a common regulator, common standards, common professional
bodies, common certification. Those write the distinctions once, for everyone in the sector — which
is why the sharing exists at all rather than being a coincidence of similar businesses.

**Prediction:** the more institutionally governed a sector, the larger the shareable fraction of its
verdict ontology. Healthcare, aviation, finance high; unregulated commercial software low.

**Falsifier:** sectors with strong shared regulation showing no more verdict-ontology sharing across
firms than unregulated ones, at matched firm size and market structure.

**Interaction:** this makes the sector verdict ontology largely a projection of institutional ground,
which routes it to Q27's trust material — a regulator is a trusted source, and its distinctions
arrive as standing supply the sector inherits rather than authors.

### (e) The boundary case — architecture straddles

**Recorded as a limit, not resolved.** Some decisions govern both: *should this be a service* is a
method decision and an output property at once. This is the same residue the delegation material
flagged as not vanishing — and it predicts where the ontologies cannot be cleanly separated, which
is useful to know in advance rather than to discover mid-session.

**Do not file (a) without (e).** A clean two-way distinction that quietly excludes its hardest cases
is the defect `DDD-frame-15` was retired for.

### (f) What the divergence between the two ontologies shows

**Proposed.** Where the act ontology and the verdict ontology do not align, three distinct
conditions arise, with three distinct symptoms:

| Divergence | Condition | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Act has a distinction the verdict lacks | **Invisible craft** | The customer has no word for it and cannot want it. Quality goes unpriced; two suppliers who differ are bought as though identical; a cost cut here is undetectable at handover |
| Verdict has a distinction the act lacks | **Unbuildable requirement** | A stated want with no technique that reaches it. The honest form of "we cannot do that" — not incapacity, but a distinction with no method attached |
| Both carve, but differently | **False agreement** | Both sides use the same word believing it names the same distinction. Agreement is reached, work proceeds, and the verdict later disagrees |

**The diagnostic value is in the asymmetry of symptoms.** The first two produce *silence* — something
unsaid, or something refused. The third produces **confident agreement that is wrong**, and nothing
in a normal engagement checks it. Disputes over delivered work are disproportionately the third kind.

**Falsifier:** competent readers, given real disputes over delivered work, classifying them into the
three conditions at no better than chance. If the types do not discriminate, they are not carving a
joint.

### (g) The conflicting term as an inspectable diff

**Proposed, and this is the instrument (f) implies.**

The third condition — one word, two carvings — was previously undetectable, because both carvings
were private and the only evidence was a dispute after the fact. When both are **declared**, the
conflict becomes a diff: the same term, two definitions, side by side, before the work starts.

This is the versioned-drift capability (Q40(d)) applied across parties rather than across time. The
same property that lets an arrangement compare its carving to last year's lets it compare its
carving to the customer's — and a term appearing in both declarations with different content is
mechanically findable.

**Two conditions on it, and they are what make it real rather than aspirational.** Both sides must
declare, which the practice can supply for its own act ontology but must *elicit* for the customer's
verdict ontology. And a matching term name is not a matching distinction: the diff finds candidates,
and adjudicating them is judgement — an assessment act with its own owner.

**Elicitation consequence, recorded here because it follows directly.** A customer saying *we are
special* is reporting a divergence from the sector's verdict ontology — the only part of their
carving they can see, because nobody states what everyone shares. It is therefore the most useful
thing they say, and the productive response is not agreement or scepticism but: **which decision does
this change?** A claimed divergence attaching to no decision is ceremonial; one attaching to many is
the engagement's scope. The ceiling from Q42 applies in reverse — the divergences they cannot name
are the dangerous ones, so their list is where elicitation starts, not where it finishes.

**Falsifier for the instrument:** declared-carving diffs over the same engagement failing to predict
where disputes later arise, against a baseline of experienced judgement without the diff.

## Interaction with delegation

The distinction explains the current position precisely, and this is worth carrying into the
G-track:

- An LLM has a **strong ambient act ontology** for software — it read the corpus of how software is
  built — and **no verdict ontology** for a specific customer's domain.
- So the supply pattern is: inherit the act ontology, mechanise only the arrangement's *divergences*
  from it (Q42), and supply the verdict ontology explicitly.
- And the act ontology is **positional** — it attaches to code structure — which is what makes it
  mechanisable through tooling at act time. The verdict ontology is not positional and cannot be
  delivered the same way.

That last point may be the reason the LSP pattern works and no equivalent exists on the verdict
side. Flagged; not claimed.

## Not canon — recorded so it does not leak

**Who owns a sector verdict ontology** is a commercial question, not a claim about determination, and
it is a live one: canon goes to a foundation, the act ontology is the practice's standing asset, and
the sector layer sits between them — most valuable, most customer-adjacent, and currently
unallocated. Section D of the 2026-08-26 addendum applies: this projects from canon and is not canon.
